# Names on the Train, Layers on the Hill

Before a graffiti piece becomes somebody’s moodboard, it is somebody’s work. Letter shape, scale, outline, placement: choices made by a writer who wants a name to hold its own in a city full of names.

The Museum of the City of New York’s City as Canvas exhibition documented that history through Martin Wong’s collection. Its paintings, sketchbooks, and photographs included work by Lee Quiñones, LADY PINK, and FUTURA 2000. The museum connects those writers’ influence to music, fashion, and visual culture. That is a much fuller story than treating graffiti as a distressed texture. [Explore the museum’s exhibition record](https://www.mcny.org/exhibition/cityascanvas).

Queens has its own important place in that story. In an interview about his practice, Meres One recalls painting at Phun Phactory in the mid-1990s, then taking over the program and naming it 5Pointz in 2002. The earlier program and the later name belong to different moments, even if they often collapse into one memory. [Read Meres One’s account](https://www.bombingscience.com/graffiti-interview-meres-one/).

Austin had a different kind of shared canvas at Castle Hill. KUT’s reporting dates the original HOPE Outdoor Gallery to 2011 and its closure to 2019, ahead of redevelopment. For people who knew the hill, its layers of paint were part of the city’s visual memory. [Read KUT’s reporting on the original site](https://www.kut.org/life-arts/2021-07-08/new-hope-outdoor-gallery-opening-date-delayed-wont-open-this-summer).

That history is still moving. HOPE now identifies its new home as 741 Dalton Lane, near the airport, with designated public painting walls and separate curated mural areas. The old Castle Hill location and today’s gallery are different places. [Check HOPE’s current visiting information](https://www.hopeoutdoorgallery.com/).

For 7TAC, respecting these scenes means naming the writers and places that inform us, supporting living artists, and giving Austin’s history its own attention. Go see the work in person. Look closely at a letter. Learn who painted it. A city becomes more interesting when you can recognize the hands that shaped it.
