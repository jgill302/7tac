# The Conversation You Almost Skipped

You can arrange a meeting in six messages and still know very little about the person you’re meeting. Sit across a table and the useful information arrives differently: a pause, a laugh, the question they ask when the prepared introduction runs out.

There is research behind taking that small social risk. In their 2014 study, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked commuters on trains and buses to either speak with someone or remain disconnected. Participants assigned to connect reported a more positive experience, even though other participants had expected solitude to be better. That finding concerns those encounters; it doesn’t make every stranger a future friend. [Read the study, Mistakenly Seeking Solitude](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/jschroeder/Publications/Epley%26Schroeder2014.pdf).

For creative work, meeting in person also lets you bring the thing itself. A print can leave its sleeve. A seam can be turned inside out. Someone can hear the difference between the version you sent and the one you are still working on. Questions come from what is actually in front of you.

We care about making those exchanges easier. Leave a little time after a meeting instead of booking the next thing immediately. Ask a maker how they solved one detail. Invite someone back to continue a conversation rather than treating the first encounter as a pitch.

And read the room. A person wearing headphones may want to keep them on. A good invitation leaves room for a comfortable no. Meeting people matters most when both people get to choose it.
